Quality Assurance

Your quality priorities are three weeks old and sorted by who escalated loudest

An agent that sweeps change controls, deviations, training, calibrations, preventive maintenance and QC samples in a single pass, and hands back the site's most pressing concerns ranked by consequence. Fast enough to be current, ranked well enough to be acted on.

What this looks like today

The site knows everything it needs to know. It just cannot say it out loud this week.

Nothing here is hidden. Every open change control, every ageing deviation, every lapsed training record and overdue calibration is sitting in a validated system with a timestamp on it. The information exists. What does not exist is a way to have it in front of you today, ordered by what actually matters, without spending a fortnight assembling it.

So the picture gets built on a monthly cycle. Extracts are requested, formats are reconciled, slides are made, and the quality council reviews a snapshot of a site that has since moved. By the time a priority list is agreed, some of it has closed on its own and something that was not on it has become the thing that matters. The review is not wrong. It is late, and late is a different kind of wrong.

The ordering is the deeper problem. Ask what the site's top five quality concerns are and the honest answer is that they are whatever escalated loudest, whatever has a due date this week, and whatever a particular manager happens to be carrying around. Systems sort by age and by volume because that is what systems can do. Nobody has the time to sort four hundred open items by consequence, so the loudest item wins and the quiet one that would actually cost you sits at position ninety.

Both failures come from the same place. Ranking by risk is slow, so it does not get done often. Being fast means ranking by age, which is not ranking at all.

The demo

Ask on a Tuesday morning, have the ranked answer before your first meeting.

The agent is asked a single question: what should this site be worried about right now. It sweeps change controls, deviations, employee training, instrument calibrations, equipment preventive maintenance and QC samples, reads the current state of each, and returns a short ranked list of the most pressing concerns with the reasoning attached to every one. Not a dashboard, not four hundred open items, and not last month's picture. A shortlist you can walk into a meeting with, generated in the time it takes to read it.

Under the hood

Speed is the cheap part. The ranking is the product.

Querying six systems quickly is a solved problem and it is not what makes this useful. Returning everything that is open, fast, produces a longer list nobody reads. The work is in deciding what belongs in the top five and being able to show why, so a QA lead can disagree with the order on the evidence rather than on instinct.

  • Six systems in one pass, current as of the moment you ask. Change controls, deviations, training, calibrations, PM and QC samples, read directly rather than through last month's extract.
  • Consequence over age. A thirty-day-old item on a product moving toward disposition outranks a two-hundred-day-old item on an idle line. Systems sort by the date because the date is what they have; the ranking here is built on what the item touches and what it would cost if it went wrong.
  • Proximity to the batch. The first question asked of every finding is whether it sits near material that is being made, tested or released right now. That single test moves most of the backlog down and a handful of items sharply up.
  • Volume does not equal signal. Thirty small training records overdue in one department is one finding, not thirty, and the agent says so. Systems that count items reward whoever generates the fewest, which is not the same as whoever carries the least risk.
  • The reasoning is exposed for every item. Each entry carries what it is, what it touches, and why it sits where it sits. A ranking you cannot interrogate is a ranking your quality lead will politely ignore.
  • Cheap enough to re-run. The value of the ordering decays over days, not months, so the sweep is built to be run before a council, before an audit, before a disposition meeting, or because someone had a bad feeling on Tuesday.

The sweep surfaces and orders. It does not open records, assign owners, classify anything, or decide that a concern is closed. Nothing it produces is a quality decision and nothing it produces should leave the site without a named human having reviewed it. What it changes is where your QA lead's week goes: onto the top three items instead of onto building the list.

What it takes to get there

Current data, and your definition of urgent rather than a generic one.

Speed depends on reading the systems rather than reading exports of them. A nightly extract makes the sweep a day stale before it starts, which is survivable, and a monthly one puts you back where you began. Read-only access to the live systems is what makes "current as of now" true instead of marketing.

The ranking depends on your definitions, not mine. Overdue, closed and current already mean different things in different systems at the same site, and what counts as high consequence depends on your products, your lines and your regulatory posture. Encode a generic risk model and you get an ordering that is confidently wrong and quietly ignored. That calibration is a working session with your QA lead, not a configuration screen.
