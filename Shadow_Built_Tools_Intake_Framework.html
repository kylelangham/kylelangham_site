Engineering Framework

When a Prototype
Lands on Your Team Handling business-built software that becomes load-bearing without engineering knowing

Kyle Langham
Consulting

A non-engineer builds something useful with an AI coding tool. It spreads. Months later it is holding up a real process, and it arrives at engineering as a fully formed obligation: undocumented, untested, and already depended on. The instinct is to gate the building in the first place, but that is the wrong lever, and pulling it results in less AI adoption across the org.

Rank by blast radius

A single-user script holding a write credential to production is more dangerous than a forty-person read-only dashboard. Four questions do the sorting instead:

Data
What does it touch?
Public, internal, confidential, regulated, or customer-identifying. This is usually decided on day one and rarely revisited.
Set early. Ask first.
Authority
What can it change?
Read-only, writes to a sandbox, writes to a system of record, or acts on the outside world (sending, paying, provisioning).
Widens quietly. Recheck.
Reversibility
How bad is wrong?
Whether a bad output is caught by a human, silently corrupts state, or leaves the building before anyone reviews it.
Widens quietly. Recheck.
Dependency
Who is relying on it?
Whether a real decision, commitment, or downstream process now assumes the output is correct and available.
Widens quietly. Recheck.

Three mechanics

Visibility, a published bar, and a credible no

Register early, review later

  • Registration is not approval. Naming a project costs the builder a paragraph and costs engineering nothing. Requiring permission to prototype pushes the work into personal accounts where you lose sight of it entirely.
  • Hang it on a request people already make. A production credential, a customer-data scope, an SSO registration, a domain, a shared account, a paid API key. Every one already routes through someone. Attach the question there and the boundary enforces itself.
  • Nobody self-declares a threshold crossing. No one thinks "I am now org-scale." Gates that depend on that judgment do not fire.
  • Run a standing intake. Thirty minutes a month. Anything others now depend on gets named. Not judged — surfaced. This alone removes the surprise, which is the actual complaint.

Publish what you will take on

  • State the bar in advance. Tests, a runbook, no undocumented production dependencies, a named owner who is not the original builder.
  • Builders aim at a published bar. Anyone who wants their tool to survive starts building toward it without being asked.
  • It depersonalizes refusal. Declining against a standard is a process. Declining case by case is a verdict on someone's work, and that is where the political damage lives.
  • Price the rewrite honestly. Adoption is a funded decision, not a formality. Silent inheritance is the most expensive outcome because nobody costed it.
The graduation decision — three outcomes, one of them chosen deliberately
Adopt
Engineering takes ownership. Meets the published bar, or the work to get there is scoped, staffed and scheduled like any other commitment.
Contain
Stays with the builder, scoped down. Narrower permissions, bounded audience, and the residual risk accepted in writing by someone senior enough to accept it.
Sunset
Retired, with the need it served either absorbed elsewhere or explicitly dropped. Rare, and it has to happen at least once to stay believable.
Contain is the answer most of the time, and it is the option most organizations do not have. Without it every decision collapses to adopt-or-abandon, adoption wins by default, and the intake process becomes a formality everyone learns to route around.

Failure modes

  • Refusal that is not real. If everything gets rescued regardless of whether it met the bar, the bar is decoration and the intake is theater within two quarters.
  • Gates without a paved road. If each tier only adds hurdles, the incentive is to stay one rung below whatever triggers review. Pair every gate with something the builder gets: a sanctioned place to build, a template with scoped credentials and logging already on.
  • Tiering by user count. It is easy to explain and it sorts the wrong things into the wrong buckets.
  • Treating the build as the problem. The organization got value from someone solving their own problem. Framing that as a governance failure teaches people to hide the next one.
Questions to answer before you write the policy
Do you want fewer tools built outside engineering, or earlier visibility into them?
Which existing request already forces a conversation you could attach this to?
Who has authority to accept residual risk on a contained tool?
What is the honest bar for adoption, stated as something a non-engineer could aim at?
Are you willing to let one visibly fail rather than absorb it?
Where does an unregistered tool get caught today, if anywhere?
