Quality Control - Invalid Assays

Your invalid assays are a pattern. You are working them one at a time.

An agent that lists every invalid result in the year and tells you which method is costing you, works a single invalid through to a draft investigation report, and ranks the assays whose system suitability margin is eroding toward the invalids they have not produced yet.

What this looks like today

Every invalid gets investigated. Nobody investigates the invalids.

An invalid run is not a failing product, it is a failing test, and that distinction is what makes it easy to absorb. The analyst repeats it, the result comes back fine, a Phase Ia record gets opened and closed, and release slips by a few days. Each one is small enough to swallow. Nobody adds them up.

So the aggregate never gets built. Asking which method produced the most invalid results this year means pulling every run from LIMS, deciding what counts as invalid versus repeated versus cancelled, joining to the lab investigations that were opened against them, and reconciling the two. It is a week of somebody's time, so it gets done for the annual review and not otherwise.

The individual investigation has the opposite problem. It is urgent, so it gets done fast by whoever is free, and the quality of the write-up tracks. Analytical evidence, disposition history, training currency and the SOP revision in force at the time sit in four systems. The pressure runs toward finding an assignable cause, because finding one lets you invalidate and move.

And the third thing does not happen at all. No one is watching a passing method lose its margin. System suitability that clears the criterion by a hair every time is a clean record, right up until it is not.

The demo

List them, work one, then find the next one before it happens.

Three passes on the same body of laboratory data. First the agent lists every invalid result recorded in 2026. Then it takes one of them and works the investigation end to end, reaching an invalidate or result-stands call and drafting the report. Then it turns the same data forward and ranks the assays whose suitability margin is trending toward the acceptance limit, which is the list of methods most likely to produce an invalid in the next six months.

Under the hood

The three questions need the same four systems and different reasoning.

Listing is a data problem. Investigating is an evidence problem. Forecasting is a trend problem. They share a substrate, which is why one agent can perform all three.

  • LIMS for every run, its result, its status and its system suitability record, including the runs that were quietly repeated without a formal invalid ever being raised. Those matter to the count and they are the ones a manual pull misses.
  • QMS for the lab investigation and its disposition, so the agent reads what was concluded at the time rather than re-deciding it, and can tell you where the same root cause has already been closed twice before.
  • eDMS for analyst training currency and the SOP revision actually in force on the date of the run. This is the check that gets skipped under pressure and it is the one that decides whether an assignable cause is real.
  • Separating a method problem from a criterion problem from an execution problem. An assay failing because the method is not robust, an assay failing because the acceptance criterion was set too tight for it, and an assay failing because of how it was run all look identical in the invalid count and need three different responses.
  • Suitability margin trended per criterion, not pass or fail. A method clearing its limit by two percent on every run has a spotless record and no headroom, and that is the signal the forward-looking pass is built on.

What it takes to get there

The count is only as good as what your LIMS calls an invalid.

Most of the difficulty here is definitional rather than technical. Sites differ on whether a repeated run is flagged, whether a cancelled sequence leaves a record, and whether system suitability is stored as a captured value or as a pass flag with the number left in the instrument software. A pass flag with no value behind it kills the forward-looking pass entirely, because there is no margin to trend. That is worth knowing before anyone builds anything, not after.

The reactive investigation needs less. If the analytical record, the QMS disposition and the training record are reachable and can be joined on something stable, it works.
